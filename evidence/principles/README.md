# Reaction principles

The four principles organize the chemistry from acceptor access to product survival. Each card links conditional design implications to attributed quotations and donor-specific applicability constraints. A principle does not replace a mechanistic experiment, and a family-level application is not proof that an individual candidate reacts.

- [RP1 — Access to a fluorinated acceptor gates nucleophile transfer](RP1.md). For an alkyl–CF3 substrate with an adjacent eliminable C–H bond, dehydrofluorination can expose a gem-difluoroalkene that accepts nucleophiles and undergoes a second fluoride loss. Increasing access to that acceptor opens a bond-forming route that a stronger nucleophile alone cannot supply. A chelation-assisted metal route is a separate activation option, not evidence that every substrate passes through the same alkene.

- [RP2 — Accessible donor speciation and capture competence must coincide](RP2.md). Deprotonation or unmasking can raise the population of a bond-forming donor, but resonance stabilization, steric shielding, ion pairing and competing reactive atoms can lower or redirect its capture. Thus, a more basic neutral donor need not be more effective in a deprotonation-dependent reaction, and changing a precursor can change performance without changing the transferred element.

- [RP3 — The state formed by capture controls fluoride loss versus fluorine retention](RP3.md). Where capture generates a suitably configured anionic or metal-associated β-fluoro intermediate, β-fluoride elimination can produce a monofluoroalkene. In compatible radical-addition systems, hydrogen-atom termination can instead trap a saturated CF2 product. Conditions that increase anionic elimination relative to H-termination can therefore improve the monofluoro branch even when they lower the yield of a competing hydroaddition product.

- [RP4 — Selective formation must outpace downstream consumption](RP4.md). When the first fluorinated product remains reactive, greater reagent exposure or stronger downstream activation can consume it by further coupling, hydrofluorination or additional C–F loss. Product accumulation therefore depends on a window that permits initial capture while suppressing subsequent transformation. This relation applies only when the downstream reaction is accessible; source-proven persistence is an explicit exception.
