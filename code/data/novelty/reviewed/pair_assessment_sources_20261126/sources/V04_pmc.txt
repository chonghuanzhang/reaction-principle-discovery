</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>β-Acyloxyl alkenyl amides (AAAs) are vinylogous esters with high activity toward acylation reactions. However, only one synthetic procedure consisting of five independent operation steps involving highly sensitive amino alkynone intermediate is available for their synthesis to date, depressing rigidly the application of AAAs in peptide synthesis and expanded acylation reactions. Herein, by combining consecutive trifold defluorination, acyl migration, and multicomponent reactions, a considerably simpler one-step protocol has been established for AAA synthesis with the α-trifluoromethyl ketones, amines, and carboxylic acids/amino acids as starting materials using Cs<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> as the only promoter. The resulting AAAs have been verified with excellent performance in the synthesis of esters, amides, and peptides. In all investigated examples of peptide synthesis, no racemization or epimerization has been observed, documenting the high application potential of AAAs as a complementary option in peptide synthesis and other related acylation reactions.</p></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract2"><hr class="headless">
<p>The α-trifluoromethyl ketone–amine was identified as a synergistic peptide coupling reagent via β-acyloxyl alkenyl amide formation.</p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p>The C─F bonds are characterized for their high bond dissociation energy, which has empowered fluorine functionalized molecules with marvelous functions (<a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1"><em>1</em></a>–<a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4"><em>4</em></a>). In addition, because of the readily availability and stability of fluorinated organic compounds, the defluorination reactions have received equivalently tremendous attention (<a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5"><em>5</em></a>–<a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8"><em>8</em></a>). Due to these extensive efforts, the defluorination reactions have exhibited substantial application in divergent organic transformations, including but not limited to asymmetrical synthesis (<a href="#R9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R9"><em>9</em></a>–<a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12"><em>12</em></a>), elimination (<a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13"><em>13</em></a>, <a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14"><em>14</em></a>), carboxylation (<a href="#R15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R15"><em>15</em></a>, <a href="#R16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R16"><em>16</em></a>), cross coupling (<a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17"><em>17</em></a>–<a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21"><em>21</em></a>), C–H activation (<a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22"><em>22</em></a>, <a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23"><em>23</em></a>), hydroperfluoroalkylation (<a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24"><em>24</em></a>), decarboxylative coupling (<a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25"><em>25</em></a>), nucleophilic substitution (<a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26"><em>26</em></a>, <a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27"><em>27</em></a>), hydrodefluorination (<a href="#R28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R28"><em>28</em></a>, <a href="#R29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R29"><em>29</em></a>), and the decomposition of polyfluoroalkyl substances (<a href="#R30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R30"><em>30</em></a>, <a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31"><em>31</em></a>). Meanwhile, compared with the transformation of a single C─F bond, the selective or multistep transformation of multiplied geminal C─F bonds brings broader space in developing distinctive organic synthesis (<a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32"><em>32</em></a>–<a href="#R34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R34"><em>34</em></a>). In this regard, exploring the defluorination reactions in those readily available trifluoromethyl compounds is promising in expanding the application of the C─F bond transformation. As a type of easily accessible and stable fluorinated compounds, the α-trifluoromethyl ketones (TFMKs) have been studied as C─F bond donors in defluorination reactions. According to reported results, the transformation on TFMKs usually starts from the elimination of one HF to form <em>gem</em>-difluoroalkene. Depending on the conditions, further one-round nucleophilic addition and HF elimination (path a; <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1A</a>) or two-round nucleophilic addition/HF elimination (path b; <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1A</a>) may take place, leading to the synthesis of fluoroalkene derivatives or functionalized enones containing no fluorine, respectively (<a href="#R35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R35"><em>35</em></a><em>–</em><a href="#R38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R38"><em>38</em></a>). Because of the narrow reaction modes, exploring different reaction pathways in TFMKs is highly desirable to expand the frontiers of C─F functionalization reactions and their synthetic application.</p>

(<strong>C</strong>) The current work on one-step synthesis of AAAs and their application.</p></figcaption></figure><p>On the other hand, the synthesis of β-acyloxyl alkenyl amides (AAAs) can be dated back to 1961 when Woodward and Olofson (<a href="#R39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R39"><em>39</em></a>) confirmed their authentic structure by revisiting Mumm’s work (<a href="#R40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R40"><em>40</em></a>) on the reactions of isoxazolium salts with sodium acetate. The value of the AAAs has attracted extensive attention when the raw carboxylic acids and amino acids have been successfully used for the synthesis of such compounds by Neuenschwander in 1968 (<a href="#R41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R41"><em>41</em></a>). In their method, the authors used the highly active amino alkynones, also termed as push-pull acetylenes (pp-acetylenes), as the nucleophilic addition acceptor. The synthesis of pp-acetylenes, as described by Neuenschwander and Hafner in the same year (<a href="#R42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R42"><em>42</em></a>), refers to a four-step process starting from commercial methyl ketones, and one more step of adding carboxylic acid to pp-acetylene leads to AAAs (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1B</a>). Although the extensive value of AAAs had been demonstrated by their efficient application in the synthesis of amides, peptides, and thiol/selenol esters (<a href="#R43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R43"><em>43</em></a>, <a href="#R44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R44"><em>44</em></a>), the fatal problems, including the extremely high sensitivity of pp-acetylenes (handled in −50° to −20°C with short storage time) (<a href="#R45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R45"><em>45</em></a>), and the tedious linear synthetic process have predetermined the inapplicability of pp-acetylenes for possible commercialization or further expanded application. Consequently, the pp-acetylene was finally abandoned without further advance until now.</p>
<p>Although more than 100 different coupling reagents have been reported for peptide synthesis since the discovery of dicyclohexyl carbodiimide (DCC) (<a href="#R46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R46"><em>46</em></a>), the heavy metal utilization, high cost, tedious linear synthetic procedure, exposure hazards, and/or occurrence of racemization/epimerization are still rigid challenges besetting peptide synthesis (<a href="#R47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R47"><em>47</em></a>–<a href="#R52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R52"><em>52</em></a>). Therefore, finding more different coupling reagents to complement these known candidates is yet the central task for the current peptide chemistry (<a href="#R53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R53"><em>53</em></a>–<a href="#R57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R57"><em>57</em></a>). Accordingly, devising facile and easy access to AAAs would reasonably bring critical advances to the peptide synthesis because of their predictable acylation reactivity resulting from the electron withdrawing effect given by the conjugate amide fragment. To address the key challenges of multistep operation and unstable intermediates in the reported method, the multicomponent reactions (MCRs) which allow the target product synthesis in one step without isolation of any intermediate would definitely be a perfect option (<a href="#R58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R58"><em>58</em></a>–<a href="#R61" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R61"><em>61</em></a>). Over the past decades, MCRs have shown dramatic application in diversity-oriented synthesis. However, because of the more critical requirement in fixed order of bond formation and structure constraint, the equivalent application of MCRs in target-oriented synthesis remains a challenge and much less developed (<a href="#R62" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R62"><em>62</em></a>–<a href="#R64" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R64"><em>64</em></a>). Herein, by making use of the consecutive functionalization of the three geminal C─F bonds in TFMKs and an acyl migration, we present a three-component, one-step method for AAA synthesis with simple reagents under mild condition. This protocol, as verified by the subsequent application in the synthesis of peptides, exhibits excellent performance by affording peptide products under mild conditions without confronting racemization or epimerization. This work conveys the discovery of the simple TFMKs and secondary amine as a synergistic coupling reagent for peptide synthesis. This work will hopefully bring opportunities to peptide chemistry and related applications (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1C</a>).</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">RESULTS</h2>
<p>First, by using α-trifluoromethyl acetophenone (<strong>1a</strong>), diethyl amine (<strong>2a</strong>), and benzoic acid (<strong>3a</strong>) as starting materials, the formation of β-benzoyl acrylamide <strong>4aa</strong> was furnished via simple operation by directly stirring in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in the presence of a base. The subsequent systematic investigation on the optimizing reaction parameters such as promoter species, medium, time, temperature, etc. revealed that a base such as Cs<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> could efficiently promote the production of <strong>4aa</strong> albeit that the product was also formed without using base. In addition, the polar solvent was favorable, and broad temperature range was tolerated. The operation by stirring at 40°C in DMSO, however, was found to provide the optimal result. Although 5.0 equiv. carboxylic acid was adopted, lower loading also could give up to 77% yield without enhancing temperature (tables S1 to S5). The method, as demonstrated by the results using different substrates, was broadly applicable to all three components with highly varied substructures (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>). The reactions of TFMK with aryl backbone gave generally satisfactory yield of products (<strong>4aa</strong>-<strong>4af</strong>), and inferior result was afforded with alkyl-based TFMKs (<strong>4ag</strong>). The reactions using different secondary amines were also practical (<strong>4ah</strong>-<strong>4aj</strong>), but primary amine such as aniline was not applicable (fig. S1A). While the reactions afforded most of the AAAs with good to excellent yields without showing regular impact from the substructure in the substrates, the relatively more sensitive diallyl amine was found to give the product with lower yield (<strong>4aj</strong>). Later, as the central aim, the reactions of diverse carboxylic acids and their functional derivatives were extensively investigated. As expected, the conventional aromatic and aliphatic acids tolerated the synthesis to give products with extraordinary efficiency (<strong>4ak</strong>-<strong>4ap</strong>). Acid natural products and pharmaceuticals such as sorbic acid, ibuprofen, flurbiprofen, ciprofibrate, oleanolic acid, and ursolic acid, tolerated also smoothly to the titled three-component assembly by affording products with good to excellent yields (<strong>4aq</strong>-<strong>4av</strong>). A more valuable fact was that the reactions of amino acids such as tryptophan, glycine, phenylalanine, proline, alanine, serine, methionine, tyrosine, isoleucine, valine, asparagine, aspartic acid, and glutamate with typical protection of Boc, Formc, Cbz, or Trt in the amino group, all led to the amino acid–derived AAAs with gratifying yields (<strong>4ba</strong>-<strong>4bn</strong>). The results in the synthesis of amino acid–derived AAAs paved the way for the later application of this activation protocol in peptide synthesis. In extended efforts, the reactions using unprotected α-amino acid, <em>N</em>-benzyl β-amino acid, or the <em>N</em>-fmoc protected phenylglycine as acid donors for the synthesis of corresponding AAAs were found impractical (fig. S1, B to D).</p>

The equally excellent result given by the AAA-based protocol resulting from rather simpler reagents supported that this protocol could be a crucial complementary option in peptide synthesis.</p>

The quick addition of benzoic acid to <strong>D</strong> gives rise to ester <strong>E</strong> (HRMS), and the acyl migration on <strong>E</strong> yields the product <strong>4a</strong>.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/F7/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></figure></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">DISCUSSION</h2>
<p>Conclusively, by using simple TFMKs and secondary amines as the reaction partners of carboxylic acids/amino acids, the three-component synthesis of AAAs, a class of excellently activated carboxylic acids, have been accessed with general substrate tolerance via mild and simple operation. The application of AAAs in the synthesis of esters, amides, as well as peptides have been investigated and exhibited excellent performance by providing products with broad scope without encountering racemization or epimerization. By offering this simple one-step protocol to replace the previously reported five-step procedure involving highly unstable intermediates, this near 60-year challenge in AAAs synthesis has been solved by combining MCRs, C─F functionalization, and acyl migration chemistry. The results also represent a convictive example of the powerful application of MCRs in target-oriented synthesis. As proven by the excellent performance of the coupling reagent in the amide, ester, and peptide synthesis, the TFMK-amine coupling tactic is expected to bring advances to peptide synthesis and other acylation reactions. Extended works on the domain are currently ongoing in the laboratory.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">MATERIALS AND METHODS</h2>
<section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">General experimental information</h3>
<p>The TFMKs (except <strong>1a</strong>) were prepared following literature processes (<a href="#R65" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R65"><em>65</em></a>, <a href="#R66" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R66"><em>66</em></a>); the TFMKs <strong>1a</strong> and all other chemicals were acquired from commercial sources and used without further treatment. All the reactions were performed under air atmosphere. The thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analysis was performed with silica gel (GF254) plate. The nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded in 400- or 600-MHz apparatus with CDCl<sub>3</sub>, DMSO-<em>d<sub>6</sub></em>, or CD<sub>3</sub>OD as solvent. The frequencies for <sup>1</sup>H NMR, <sup>13</sup>C NMR, and <sup>19</sup>F NMR analysis were 400/600, 100/150, 376/565 MHz, respectively. The coupling of peaks was marked as s (singlet), d (doublet), t (triplet), q (quartet), m (multiplet), and dd (doublet of doublet). The chemical shifts were reported in parts per million with tetramethyl silane (TMS) as internal standard. HRMS data were acquired in a mass spectrometer equipped with time-of-flight analyzer under electrospray ionization mode. Melting points were rested in an X-4A apparatus without correcting temperature. The chiral high-performance liquid chromatography analysis were run in an apparatus equipped with Shimadzu LC 40D XS analyzer. The optical rotation was recorded in a JH-P300 polarimeter. The crystal analysis was carried out on a Rigaku Oxford SuperNova diffractometer.</p></section><section id="sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">General procedure for the three-component AAAs synthesis</h3>
<p>To a 10-ml standard tube were added TFMK <strong>1</strong> (0.2 mmol), carboxylic acid <strong>3</strong> (1.0 mmol), Cs<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> (0.3 mmol) in DMSO (2 ml) or <em>N</em>,<em>N</em>′-dimethylformamide (DMF)/H<sub>2</sub>O (3/2 ml), and then diethylamine <strong>2</strong> (0.3 mmol). The resulting mixture was stirred at 40°C with oil bath heating for proper time as noted in <a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>. After being cooled down to room temperature, 5 ml of water was added, and the resulting mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate (EA; 3 × 10 ml). The organic phases were collected and washed with a small amount of water (for reaction using simple carboxylic acids) or saturated Na<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> solution (for reactions using amino acids) for three times. After being dried with anhydrous Na<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>, the solid was removed by filtration. The solvent in the resulting solution was removed at reduced pressure. The residue obtained therein was subjected to flash silica gel column chromatography to provide pure products with the elution of mixed petroleum ether (PE) and EA (v/v = 1:1 to 5:1), or mixed PE, EA, and triethylamine (v/v/v = 14:7:1 to 20:1:1).</p></section><section id="sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">General procedure for the synthesis of amides <strong>6a</strong>-<strong>6o</strong>
</h3>
<p>To a 10-ml sealed tube were added enol AAA <strong>4</strong> (0.2 mmol), amine <strong>5</strong> (0.24 mmol), and EA (1 ml) under air. The mixture was then stirred at 30°C for 3 to 24 hours. Upon completion, the mixture was transferred into a round-bottom flask, and solvent was removed at reduced pressure. The residue obtained therein was subjected to flash silica gel column chromatography to provide pure products <strong>6</strong> with the elution of mixed PE and EA (v/v = 1:2 to 10:1).</p></section><section id="sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">General procedure for the synthesis of esters <strong>6p</strong>-<strong>6q</strong>
</h3>
<p>To a 10-ml sealed tube were added AAA <strong>4</strong> (0.3 mmol), phenol/benzyl alcohol (0.75 mmol), 1,8-diazabicyclo[5.4.0]undec-7-ene (DBU; 0.3 mmol), and EtOAc (1.0 ml). The mixture was stirred at 100°C for 6 hours. Upon completion, the mixture was transferred into round-bottom flask and subjected to reduced pressure to remove the solvent. The residue was used to flash silica gel column chromatography to provide pure products <strong>6o-6p</strong> with the elution of mixed PE and EA (v/v = 100:1).</p></section><section id="sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">General procedure for the synthesis of dipeptides <strong>8</strong> with AAAs</h3>
<p>To a 10-ml sealed tube were added AAA <strong>4</strong> (0.1 mmol), amino acid <strong>7</strong> (0.12 mmol), and EA (1 ml). The mixture was then stirred at 30°C for 5 to 24 hours. Upon completion, the mixture was transferred into a round-bottom flask, and solvent was removed at reduced pressure. The residue obtained therein was subjected to flash silica gel column chromatography to provide pure products with the elution of mixed PE and EA (v/v = 1:4 to 10:1).</p></section><section id="sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">General procedure for the one-pot, two-step synthesis of dipeptides <strong>8</strong>
</h3>
<section id="sec11"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">
Method A
</h4>
<p>To a 10-ml standard tube were added TFMK <strong>1</strong> (0.2 mmol), amino acid <strong>3</strong> (1.0 mmol), Cs<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> (0.3 mmol), DMSO (2 ml), and diethylamine <strong>2a</strong> (0.3 mmol). The resulting mixture was stirred at 40°C with oil bath heating for 1.5 to 5 hours. After the TFMKs was fully consumed, amino acid <strong>7</strong> (0.24 mmol) was added to the reaction mixture and further stirred at room temperature until the activated enol esters was fully consumed. Upon completion, 5 ml of water was added, and the resulting mixture was extracted with EA (3 × 10 ml). The organic phases were collected and washed with a small amount of saturated sodium bicarbonate solution for five times. After being dried with anhydrous Na<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>, the solid was removed by filtration. The solvent in the resulting solution was removed at reduced pressure. The residue obtained therein was subjected to flash silica gel column chromatography to provide pure products with the elution of mixed PE and EA (v/v = 1:1 to 10:1).</p></section><section id="sec12"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">
Method B
</h4>
<p>To a 10-ml standard tube were added TFMK <strong>1</strong> (0.2 mmol), amino acid <strong>3</strong> (1.0 mmol), Cs<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> (0.3 mmol), DMF (3 ml)/H<sub>2</sub>O (2 ml), and diethylamine <strong>2a</strong> (0.3 mmol). The resulting mixture was stirred at 40°C with oil bath heating for 4 to 12 hours. After the TFMKs was fully consumed, 5 ml of water was added, and the resulting mixture was extracted with EA (3 × 10 ml). The organic phases were collected and washed with a small amount of water for three times. After being dried with anhydrous Na<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>, the solid was removed by filtration and the solvent in the resulting solution was removed at reduced pressure. Then, 1 ml of EA was added to the reaction mixture to resolve the residue. Subsequently, amino acid <strong>7</strong> (0.24 mmol, 1.2 equiv) was added to the mixture and further stirred at room temperature until the activated enol esters was fully consumed. After that, 5 ml of water was added, and the resulting mixture was extracted with EA (3 × 10 ml). The organic phases were collected and washed with a small amount of saturated sodium bicarbonate solution for five times. After being dried with anhydrous Na<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>, the solid was removed by filtration. The solvent in the resulting solution was removed at reduced pressure. The residue obtained therein was subjected to flash silica gel column chromatography to provide pure products with the elution of mixed PE and EA (v/v = 1:1 to 10:1).</p></section></section><section id="sec13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Procedure for the synthesis of tripeptide <strong>15</strong>
</h3>
<section id="sec14"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">
Synthesis of <strong>9</strong>
</h4>
<p>To a 15-ml test tube were added <strong>4bi</strong> (1 mmol) and amino acid <strong>7e</strong> (1.1 mmol) in EA (8 ml). After stirring at 50°C for 12 hours, the mixture was moved to a round-bottom flask to remove the solvent under reduced pressure. The residue was subjected to silica gel chromatography to provide <strong>9</strong> as colorless liquid with mixed PE and EA (v/v = 8: 1) as eluent.</p></section><section id="sec15"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">
Synthesis of <strong>10</strong>
</h4>
<p>To a 15-ml test tube were added <strong>9</strong> (1 mmol), trifluoroacetic acid (1 ml), AcOH (1 ml), and dichloromethane (DCM) (3 ml). The mixture was stirred at 40°C for 18 hours. Upon completion, the mixture was neutralized with saturated NaHCO<sub>3</sub> solution and extracted with EA (3× 10 ml). The organic phase was combined and dried with anhydrous Na<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>. After filtration, the solution was subjected to reduced pressure to remove the solvent. The residue was subjected to silica gel chromatography to provide <strong>10</strong> with mixed DCM and MeOH as eluent (v/v = 35:1).</p></section><section id="sec16"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">
Synthesis of <strong>11</strong>
</h4>
<p>To a 15-ml test tube were added <strong>10</strong> (1 mmol), Cs<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> (3 mmol), DMF/H<sub>2</sub>O (6/4 ml), TFMK <strong>1a</strong> (2 mmol), and diethyl amine (3 mmol). The mixture was stirred at 50°C for 1 hour and then neutralized with saturated NaHCO<sub>3</sub> solution. The resulting mixture was extracted wit EA (3 × 10 ml), washed with brine, and dried with anhydrous Na<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>. After filtration, the solution was subjected to reduced pressure to remove the solvent. The residue was subjected to silica gel chromatography to provide <strong>11</strong> with the elution of mixed PE and EA (v/v = 1:1).</p></section><section id="sec17"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">
Synthesis of <strong>12</strong>
</h4>
<p>To a 15-ml test tube were added <strong>11</strong> (1 mmol) and amino acid <strong>7f</strong> (1.1 mmol) in EA (8 ml). After stirring at 50°C for 12 hour. The mixture was moved to a round-bottom flask to remove the solvent under reduced pressure. The residue was subjected to silica gel chromatography to provide <strong>12</strong> as colorless liquid with mixed PE and EA (v/v = 3:2) as eluent.</p></section><section id="sec18"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">
Synthesis of <strong>13</strong>
</h4>
<p>To a 15-ml test tube were added <strong>12</strong> (1 mmol), trifluoroacetic acid (1 ml), AcOH (1 ml), and DCM (3 ml). The mixture was stirred at 40°C for 12 hour. Upon completion, the mixture was neutralized with saturated NaHCO<sub>3</sub> solution and extracted with EA (3 × 10 ml). The organic phase was combined and dried with anhydrous Na<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>. After filtration, the solution was subjected to reduced pressure to remove the solvent. The residue was subjected to silica gel chromatography to provide <strong>13</strong> with mixed DCM and MeOH as eluent (v/v = 35:1).</p></section><section id="sec19"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">
Synthesis of <strong>14</strong>
</h4>
<p>To a 15-ml test tube were added <strong>13</strong> (0.5 mmol), DMF/H<sub>2</sub>O (3/2 ml), Cs<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> (1.5 mmol), TFMK <strong>1a</strong> (1 mmol), and diethyl amine (1.5 mmol). The mixture was stirred at 50°C for 1 hour and then neutralized with saturated NaHCO<sub>3</sub> solution. The resulting mixture was extracted wit EA (3 × 10 ml), washed with brine, and dried with anhydrous Na<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>. After filtration, the solution was subjected to reduced pressure to remove the solvent. The residue was subjected to silica gel chromatography to provide <strong>11</strong> with the elution of mixed PE and EA (v/v = 1:1).</p></section><section id="sec20"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">
Synthesis of <strong>15</strong>
</h4>
<p>To a 15-ml test tube were added <strong>14</strong> (0.1 mmol), amino acid <strong>7a</strong> (0.11 mmol), and EA (1 ml). The mixture was stirred at 50°C for 12 hours. Upon completion, the mixture was subjected to reduced pressure to remove the solvent. The residue was then subjected to silica gel column chromatography to give product <strong>15</strong> with the elution of mixed EA and PE (v/v = 3:1).</p></section></section></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
